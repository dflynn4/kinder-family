Mom's Side: the Deyes

Alan's mother's people. An old New Jersey family with a Dutch-colonial name that settled the Ohio farm country, married into an English Quaker line with a Civil War soldier in it, and drifted west a state at a time until a schoolteacher married a farmer in Walla Walla.

The schoolteacher

Alan's mother was Vera May Deye. She was born around 1899 in Gladwin County, Michigan, up in the middle of the state Probable. By the time she was grown, the family had moved clear across the country to Walla Walla, Washington, where Vera became a schoolteacher. On October 15, 1923, she married a young wheat farmer from nearby Waitsburg named Darrell Kinder (his family is on the other page) Proven. Alan came along two years later. Vera lived a long life and died in 1995 at 96.

Their marriage license still exists, and it's a little goldmine, because on one page, in the couple's own answers, it confirms both families at once. It lists Darrell, farmer of Waitsburg, son of James B. Kinder and Ella Muncy. And it lists Vera, teacher, born in Michigan, daughter of Charles Deye of Ohio and Lou North of Iowa. Two whole generations, nailed down by one 1923 document.

Not a German name after all

Here's a correction worth being honest about. The easy assumption, and the one I made at first, was that "Deye" is a German name, because Vera's father came out of Shelby County, Ohio, a county full of German farmers. But when we actually chased the records back, the family turned out to be something else: an old colonial family out of New Jersey, not German at all.

Vera's father, Charles William Deye, was born on May 16, 1868, in Shelby County, Ohio, and lived until 1946 Proven. His father was Jacob Dey, born in Shelby County, Ohio, in 1834 Proven. Notice the spelling: the older gravestone reads "Dey," and the kids later softened it to "Deye."

And now we can name the man who brought the family to Ohio. Jacob's father was John Wesley Dey, born January 10, 1810, in New Jersey, who moved out to the Ohio farm country and died there in 1851 Probable (three separate records agree, including his gravestone in the very same Ohio cemetery as his son Jacob). And behind him, probably, his own father: Lewis Dey, born in 1779 in Freehold, New Jersey, who married a Mary Baird Probable. So Mom's side runs back to New Jersey in the 1700s.

How much further? The Deys were one of those old New Jersey families that had been in America since colonial times, back when the name got spelled a dozen different ways. But here's where honesty matters: the internet can't even agree where they first came from, some trees say a Dutch settler, some say a Danish one who landed in 1639, and the deep chain back to any of them isn't actually proven for Alan's branch. So the solid story is "an old New Jersey family, here since the 1600s," and the rest is a lead to chase, not a fact.

The other half: a Civil War soldier and a Quaker line

Vera's mother was Lou May North, born on October 15, 1876, in Hampton, Iowa (Vera's middle name, "May," came straight from her) Proven. This side reaches back the furthest of anyone on Alan's mom's side, and it has the best story on it.

Lou's father was Daniel Martin North, born on January 6, 1843, in Indiana Proven. He was a Civil War soldier, and the Iowa army roster still has his exact line: he enlisted at age 19 out of Hampton, Iowa, in August 1862, and served as a corporal in Company H of the 32nd Iowa Infantry Proven. He didn't go alone, either. Right below him on that same roster is a John W. North, also 19, same town, enlisted the same day, almost certainly his brother, maybe his twin Probable. Their regiment saw brutal fighting. At the Battle of Pleasant Hill in Louisiana in 1864 it was completely surrounded, with nearly half its men killed or wounded, and had to cut its way out. It fought again at the Battle of Nashville that December, and finished the war helping storm the Confederate forts at Mobile, Alabama in April 1865. Daniel came home, farmed in Iowa, and raised his family there.

Daniel had a rough start himself: his mother, Maria Howe, died when he was only four months old, and he was raised by his grandmother Probable. His father was Layton North, born December 11, 1809, in Ohio, who married Maria in Indiana in 1838 and died young in 1851 Proven (his gravestone and marriage record both survive). One more step back, probably, are Layton's own parents, John North and Tamar Mendenhall Probable, an old English Quaker family that came down the classic Quaker trail from Pennsylvania through the Carolinas and into Ohio and Indiana.

And here's a tantalizing thread, told straight. That name, Mendenhall, belongs to one of the best-documented Quaker families in America, with real church records running all the way back to a Benjamin Mendenhall who sailed from Wiltshire, England, and settled near Philadelphia in the 1680s, born back in 1662. If Tamar Mendenhall connects into that family, and she very likely does, then Alan's line would reach clear back to 1600s England on solid Quaker records. But it hangs on one missing piece: nobody has yet found the record naming Tamar's own father. So this stays a strong maybe, a real lead worth chasing, not something to carve in stone. It's the single most promising open door on the whole family tree.

The path west

Put it together and Alan's mom's family made the slow American drift, about one state per generation. The Deyes came from New Jersey to Ohio around 1830. Charles Deye then moved to Iowa, where he married Lou North, whose Quaker-stock family had worked their way over from Indiana. The young couple went up to the Michigan woods, where their daughters, Vera included, were born in the 1890s. And by the early 1900s the whole family had gone all the way out to Walla Walla, Washington. There, their schoolteacher daughter married into the Kinder wheat-ranching family, and Alan's two sides, the Oregon Trail Kinders and the New Jersey Deyes, finally met.

How far back it really goes

The Deye name is proven back to Jacob Dey, born 1834 in Ohio, and probably two more steps, to his father John Wesley Dey (1810, New Jersey) and grandfather Lewis Dey (1779, Freehold, New Jersey). The North line is the deepest and most promising branch on the whole family: proven to Layton North (1809, Ohio), probably to John North and Tamar Mendenhall, English Quakers, and with a real shot, if one record turns up, at reaching Quaker Wiltshire, England, in the 1600s.
